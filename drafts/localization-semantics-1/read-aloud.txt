Localization Semantics I. Atoms and probes: equality, types and loops in combinatory logic, tree calculus and interaction nets.

By Maxine Levesque and Claude. Claude (Opus 5.5) drafted the prose and the figures and wrote the Lean proofs; Maxine Levesque set the questions and the framework, and made every editorial decision. Every review pass was run independently by GPT-6.2-Sol.

This is the read-aloud version of the report. The written version, with interactive figures and notes, is at maxine.science/research/localization-semantics-1.

Abstract. A type can be given by rules for building a term or by tests applied to what the term does. This report develops the second view into a framework, localization semantics. A calculus is presented by descriptions built from atoms, steps between descriptions, and an observation: here, probes that run a term in a context and read back an answer. Terms joined by steps form fibres, and a type is a predicate that no step changes, so it holds of a whole fibre or of none of it. The examples are in combinatory logic, and selected figures also have tree-calculus or interaction-net views. The report compares three equalities on terms, shows what a type cannot see inside a fibre (how long a run is, whether two runs count as one, whether the order of evaluation reaches a value), and shows what changes when probes may read code. It ends by setting localization semantics beside operational, denotational and observational semantics. Many of its claims are mechanized in Lean, and the text says which, and where a claim is argued by hand.

In the written version, each claim that has been checked in the Lean proof assistant is underlined and links to its proof. Those links are not read here; where a claim has been argued by hand and not mechanized, the text says so.

How terms are read aloud. Terms are read letter by letter. Two terms side by side are the first applied to the second, and applications group to the left. A group that does not begin the term starts with the word ‘bracket’ and ends with the words ‘close bracket’, which are left unsaid when the group runs to the end of the term. So ‘K a bracket I b’ is K a applied to I b, and ‘S x y z steps to x z bracket y z’ is the S-rule. The capital letters S, K and I are always constants, and the other letters are always variables. The one constant of tree calculus is read as ‘leaf’, and the Greek letter omega as ‘omega’. The hole in a probe’s context is read as ‘the hole’, and the arrow of a type as ‘to’.

Typing rules say how a program of a given type is built. A semantic type, which is a predicate on terms, tests what the program does when it is used. Proofs of type soundness go from the first to the second: a program built by the rules is safe to run, or passes the tests of its type, shown by syntactic means or with types as predicates. A note cites Wright and Felleisen for the syntactic method, and Milner, and for a recent use at scale the RustBelt work of Jung, Jourdan, Krebbers and Dreyer, for types as predicates.

This essay explains a framework, localization semantics, that separates how a term is written from what it does when used, as the two accounts of typing do for typed programs. Atoms make up the text of a term, and probes test what the term does. In full these are description atoms and behaviour probes. The distinction is easy to miss, because a probe is itself written with atoms, and the two look alike on the page. Steps run a term, and they sit between the two. The framework calls running computation.

The distinction bears on choices that a language designer makes. It shows which equalities a type checker can decide by running terms and which have to be proved. It shows how an operator that inspects code tells apart terms that application treats alike, which costs abstraction. It names what a type does not record, such as the cost of a program, and it places the usual notion of computation among several.

Each ingredient keeps one colour through all the figures. Ochre marks descriptions, the atoms and the text made from them. Green marks steps and runs. Blue marks observation, which covers probes, what they read, and evidence. Black marks what is kept, the line and its points. Each point stands for a group of terms counted as equal, and the line sets the groups side by side. Red marks a leak or a clash. A filled green button starts a run.

Combinatory logic is the calculus of the examples. Its terms are built by application from the constants S, K and I and from variables, and its three rules are given in the section on atoms and probes.

Tree calculus has one constant, the leaf, and five rules. The K-rule is: leaf leaf y z steps to y. The S-rule is: leaf bracket leaf x close bracket y z steps to x z bracket y z. Three rules of triage choose by the shape of the third argument: leaf bracket leaf w x close bracket y leaf steps to w; leaf bracket leaf w x close bracket y bracket leaf u steps to x u; and leaf bracket leaf w x close bracket y bracket leaf u v steps to y u v. These are the rules of the tree calculus specification, where they are called triage calculus. J. Bader suggested them as an alternative to the original rules of B. Jay; a note cites his Reflective Programs in Tree Calculus, 2021. A term with no redex anywhere is a normal form. Every closed normal form is a leaf, a stem, leaf u, or a fork, leaf u v, with u and v normal forms as well. With variables there are other normal forms, such as the variable a, and a applied to leaf. Tree reduction is confluent. The figures write K for leaf leaf, I for leaf bracket leaf K close bracket leaf, S x y for leaf bracket leaf x close bracket y, and omega for S I I, so that the terms read as in combinatory logic.

Interaction nets are due to Y. Lafont; a note cites his ‘Interaction nets’, 1990. The eager table of tree-semantics, the companion Lean repository on tree calculus and interaction nets, compiles a term to a net of agents. The table has fifteen rules, and its agents stand for the leaf, the stem and the fork, and for applying, triage, copying and erasing. For closed trees, tree-semantics proves that if a tree applied to another evaluates strictly to a value, the compiled net has a run to an irreducible net that reads back that value. A step is one interaction, between two agents joined at their principal ports. The table is eager, so a net runs an argument even when that argument will be discarded. In the figure of running, under ‘One step’, the thick green wire joins the two agents about to interact, and the label ‘result’ marks the result port. The figures name each net by the term read back at its result port, and a variable that a probe supplies appears as a free wire. The browser’s model compiles and reads back such open terms, and the proofs do not cover them.

Section: Two accounts of a typed program.

A type can be given by rules for building a term, or by tests applied to a finished one. The rest of the essay uses the two judgments that follow and the set called the semantic type A. How a derivation is built matters less here, and readers who do not know typing judgments can pass over those details.

Syntactic typing.

The judgment is written gamma, a turnstile, t, a colon, and A. Read: under the assumptions gamma, the term t has type A. A derivation proves the statement. It is built from rules, one for each way of forming a term, and arguments about it go by induction on the derivation.

Semantic typing.

The judgment is written t, the sign of membership, and A in double square brackets. Read: t belongs to the set of terms that pass the tests for A, a predicate on terms. A term belongs to the semantic type A to B if applying it to any member of the semantic type A yields a member of the semantic type B.

The two are connected by the fundamental lemma of logical relations, which states that every typing rule preserves membership in the semantic type. A closed term that can be derived therefore passes every test that its type imposes. Under assumptions gamma the same holds once each variable is replaced by a term that passes the tests of its assumed type. A note cites Tait, Plotkin and Statman.

The framework applies the same division to terms. A term considered as a piece of text is called a description. It is the tree obtained by grouping the term’s symbols, as a parser does, and two descriptions are equal only if they are the same tree. Descriptions are made of atoms. What a term does when it is used is a separate matter, and probes find it out.

Section: Atoms and probes.

The examples are written in combinatory logic, introduced by Schönfinkel and developed systematically by Curry and Feys; notes cite them. The version used here has three constants and no binding of variables. Two terms written side by side form an application of the first to the second, and applications group to the left, so that S K K a means S applied to K, the result applied to K, and that applied to a. The constants are defined by three rules: I x steps to x, K x y steps to x, and S x y z steps to x z bracket y z. In a functional language I is the identity function and K is const, and S passes one argument to two functions and applies the first result to the second. A term is run by using the rules until none applies. In the rules, x, y and z stand for any terms. In the terms themselves the letters a, b, x and y are variables, which have no rules of their own, and omega abbreviates S I I.

An atom is a node of syntax. Here the atoms are the constants, the variables, and application. A term is a tree of atoms, and to ask about its atoms is to ask what the text consists of. A programmer would call this the white-box view.

A probe is an experiment on a term, the black-box view. The probe supplies a context with a hole, such as the hole applied to x and y, whose variables are fresh, new names that occur nowhere in the term under test. A probe is run only on a term without variables of its own, such as each of the six terms of the figure, so every variable in an outcome came from the probe. The term is put in the hole and the result is run. The probe then reads the outcome, but it may read only what it supplied. If the outcome is written entirely in the probe’s own variables, as x y is, the probe reads it. If the outcome still contains S, K or I, those symbols are part of the term’s text, and the probe may not look at them. It can try another context instead, for instance one that supplies another argument. Under the hole applied to x, the term I gives x, which the probe reads. The term K I gives I, which still contains a constant, so the probe does not read it; under the hole applied to x and y the same term gives y, which it reads. When the outcome cannot be read, the figure says ‘not read’, and a run that never reaches a normal form gives no reading either. A term with variables of its own, such as K a, is not put in a probe directly. It is compared through its closed instances, the terms obtained by replacing each of its variables by a term without variables. A variable stands for any term, so two such terms are the same under probes when every replacement of their variables by terms without variables, made in both at once, gives two terms that no probe tells apart. Two simpler rules fail. If a probe could read the term’s own variables, a I and a K would be the same under probes, since neither can ever be read, yet they come apart once I replaces a. If instead a probe were run on the term itself, with every outcome containing those variables unreadable, it would have to discard a before reading anything, and a would look like a term with no value. For terms with no variables of their own, such as these six, comparing their closed instances is the same as reading them directly, and the stricter rule gives the same readings, since such a term puts no variable of its own into an outcome. This restriction is a decision about observation. It does not follow from the three rules, and it can be changed while they stay as they are. The probes follow Morris in comparing terms in contexts; here only outcomes written in the probe’s own variables are readable. A note adds that the definition follows J. H. Morris’s thesis of 1968, Lambda-Calculus Models of Programming Languages, which compares terms by placing them in contexts. Here the observation is a reading built only from the context’s own variables, a variant suited to a calculus with free variables, and terms with variables are compared through their closed instances.

Two terms without variables are the same under every probe if no context, however elaborate and however many times it uses the term, leads to different readings. Two terms with variables are the same if this holds of the pair of closed instances given by each replacement of their variables. The figure tries three simple contexts on six terms. For these six terms, agreement in the figure implies agreement under every probe.

In interaction nets the browser compiles each experiment to a net and runs it by interactions, and the readings are those of tree calculus. Each cell also shows, as a small number, how many interactions its run took in the browser’s reducer. These cells contain free wires, and their readings and counts are the browser’s and are not proved. Each count is of one run, in one order. In a well-formed net, under a well-formed table with at most one rule for each pair of agents, two interactions either give the same net or lead to a common net in one more interaction each. When two different nets result, this is a diamond. In general, if a term that steps to two different terms always has each of them step, in one step, to a common term, then any two runs from one term that end in normal forms have the same length and end at the same normal form. So, as Lafont observed in ‘Interaction combinators’, 1997, under those conditions any two runs from the same net to irreducible nets take the same number of interactions. The browser’s open nets are not covered by that proof. The count is not part of the reading, and the points group the rows by their readings alone. The figure shows two groups of rows that agree. The three identities, I, S K K and S K S, each return their argument, since I x, S K K x and S K S x all run to x. However a context copies or discards such a term, each copy that is applied returns what it is given, whichever identity it is. A copy left unapplied in the outcome puts a constant there, and then the probe may not read the outcome at all. So the three give the same reading in every context. K I and K bracket S K K agree for the same reason, since each discards its first argument and leaves an identity.

Application is the plainest case of a symbol with two roles. Read as text, S K K x is a tree with an application node at its root, and that node is an atom like any other. Read as an experiment, the same expression is the probe, the hole applied to x, used on S K K.

The checkbox lifts the restriction. With it ticked, a probe runs as before and may then read the whole normal form it reaches, constants included, and the six rows separate.

The written version sets atoms and probes side by side in a table. What each is: an atom is a node of syntax; a probe is a context with a hole, and a rule for what may be read. The question each answers: for atoms, what does the text consist of; for probes, what does the term do when used. Equality: for atoms, the same tree; for probes, the same readings under every probe. Method: for atoms, induction on the text; for probes, extensionality: terms are equal if every use of them gives equal results.

Type theory already has names for the atoms that do each job. An introduction form is an atom that builds a value of a type, and an elimination form is an atom that builds a probe for one. A pair is built by pairing and tested by asking for either component. A function is built by abstraction, written lambda, and tested by applying it to an argument, so application is an atom of the text and also the way a probe uses a term. A note adds that in a functional language the constructors and lambda are introduction forms, and application, projection and pattern matching are elimination forms. A positive type, such as a sum or the natural numbers, is determined by its introduction forms. A negative type, such as a function or a record, is determined by its eliminations. It cites Andreoli, Levy and Zeilberger, and, for a language in which negative types are defined by their observations, the copatterns of Abel, Pientka, Thibodeau and Setzer.

Section: Running a term.

Between writing a term and probing it comes running it. A part of a term that matches the left side of a rule is a redex. A step replaces one redex by the right side of its rule, and a run, which the framework calls computation, is a sequence of steps. A term with no redex is a normal form, and when a run ends in a normal form, that normal form is a value of the term the run began with. A note points to Plotkin’s structural operational semantics for a small-step presentation, and to Kahn’s natural semantics for a big-step one.

A term can have more than one redex, and so more than one run. The term K a bracket I b has two runs that go all the way to a normal form. The K-rule can be used at once, which discards I b and gives a in one step. Or I b can be run first, which gives K a b and then a, in two steps. The term a is a normal form, because a variable has no rule, and so it is the value of both runs. In combinatory logic any two finite runs from the same term can be continued until they meet, a property called confluence; a note cites Huet and Lévy, ‘Computations in orthogonal rewriting systems’. A term therefore has at most one value, though its runs may differ in length.

Section: Three equalities.

The figure of fourteen terms has boxes for descriptions, arrows for steps, and a line of points underneath. Once the atoms and the rules are chosen, the boxes and arrows are fixed. The line records which descriptions count as the same, and that question has three reasonable answers.

As in combinatory logic, the fibre of a is kept apart from the loop by closed instances, by an argument not mechanized for trees: the loop has no variables, so if a agreed with it, the loop would agree both with I and with K, which the probe, the hole applied to x and y, tells apart. In interaction nets the figure keeps these tree terms. The first figure ran their probes as nets in the browser, with the same readings.

The three are named by what decides them, and each name keeps its colour for the rest of the essay. Sameness of text, the equality that atoms give, identifies two descriptions only when they are the same tree. Sameness after running identifies descriptions that lie in one fibre, joined to one another by steps, and it is the only one of the three defined by running. In a confluent calculus in which every term has a value, two terms lie in one fibre exactly when they run to the same value, so this equality can be decided by running both sides. An equality decided by running in this way is one source of what a proof assistant calls definitional equality. Sameness under probes identifies descriptions that no probe can tell apart, and it is a form of what is usually called contextual or observational equivalence. Morris and Plotkin, cited in notes, give other instances of the contextual test. A note cites, for definitional equality in one proof assistant, as reduction together with eta and further rules such as proof irrelevance, the Lean language reference.

Terms joined by steps agree under every probe, so sameness after running implies sameness under probes. The reason is that a step inside a term is still a step when the term sits in a context. So if t steps to u, a probe’s run on t can begin by taking that step in each copy of t in the context, and continue as a run on u. A step is also still a step when the same terms replace the variables on both sides, so for terms with variables the argument applies to each pair of closed instances. Confluence is the property that two runs from one term can always be continued until they meet. By it, if the experiment on either term has a finite run to a normal form the probe can read, the experiment on the other has a run to that same normal form, and the probe reads the same thing from both.

The converse fails, and the terms I, S K K and S K S show it. In the table of six terms and three probes, in the section on atoms and probes, the three had the same readings in every column, so sameness under probes puts them on one point. Each is also a normal form already, and by confluence two different normal forms never lie in one fibre, so sameness after running keeps them apart. The other points under probes are placed by fibre. The fibre of a stands apart from the loop because a closed instance separates them: with I in place of a, the probe, the hole applied to x, reads x from it and nothing from the loop.

Where sameness under probes holds and running does not show it, the equality has to be proved. With addition defined by recursion on its first argument, zero plus x steps to x, but x plus zero does not step at all, because the recursion is waiting on the variable x. The two sides of x plus zero equals x agree under every probe, yet no step joins them. In a proof assistant such an equation is stated as a proposition, an equality type, and here it has to be proved, by induction on x. An equality stated in this way is called propositional. A compiler’s optimiser works with sameness under probes from the other side. It may replace a term by any other over the same point, and it looks for one with a shorter run.

A rule can be added to bring sameness after running closer to sameness under probes. In combinatory logic, tick the box in the figure. The rule S K y steps to I is sound: for every y without variables, S K y and I agree under every probe, and so for every y, since each closed instance of S K y is S K applied to a term without variables. Applied to an argument, S K y x runs to x in two steps, whatever y is. With the rule added, S K K and S K S step to I, and their three separate points become one, as they were under probes. The argument given for the three identities in the section on atoms and probes applies to S K y as it does to them. In tree calculus the same rule is unsound, as the paragraph on tree calculus after the figure explains.

The eta-rule of the lambda calculus is a rule of this kind. In the lambda calculus, lambda x dot t is the function that takes an argument x and returns t, and combinatory logic can be read as the lambda calculus with these bound variables compiled away. The eta-rule makes a function f interchangeable with lambda x dot f x, the function that takes a fresh x and returns f applied to x. It says that a function is no more than what it returns when applied, which is the black-box view of the section on atoms and probes. A note cites Barendregt, and Jay and Ghani on eta as an expansion, and recalls that Schönfinkel introduced the combinators in order to remove bound variables. When the calculus gives the terms of a type such a rule, a checker decides by running what would otherwise need a proof. Function and record types, which are known by their probes, have one in Lean and in Agda. Sums and the natural numbers have laws of the same kind, which Lean does not use as steps and leaves to be proved. A note adds that Lean’s definitional equality includes eta for functions and for structures, types with a single constructor that are neither recursive nor indexed, and so not for sums or the natural numbers. Agda gives record types eta by default, except coinductive records. Altenkirch, Dybjer, Hofmann and Scott give a normalization result for one typed calculus with the eta-law for sums, which shows how much machinery that law needs there.

Section: Types.

Any property of terms is a predicate on descriptions, and it can be drawn as shading on the boxes of the figure of fourteen terms. The property of mentioning K is such a predicate. It would make a poor type, because K a bracket K a has the property and the term a, to which it steps, does not. A program would belong to the type before a step and not after it. A predicate of this kind leaks.

In this essay a type is a predicate that does not leak, so that no step leads from a term that satisfies it to a term that does not, in either direction. A fibre, as before, is a set of terms joined to one another by steps, and a predicate that does not leak shades each fibre entirely or not at all. It can therefore be stated by naming points on the line, when the line is drawn for sameness after running and has one point for each fibre. The framework’s word is behaviour, which is slightly wider. A behaviour may attach evidence to a term, and not only a yes or a no, and a predicate is a behaviour exactly when it does not leak. Where the evidence is a yes or a no, the two notions coincide.

The accounts of types as predicates cited in the note each have a condition of this sort, and the conditions differ. Some require every run of a member to end. The term K a bracket omega omega, where omega omega is the loop of the section on running, can step straight to a or run its argument for ever, so it fails the condition that every run end, while a, which is one step away, meets it. The types here are closed under steps in both directions, and the fundamental lemma later in this section uses closure under steps taken backwards. A note sets out the conditions it compares. In a standard saturated-set formulation of Tait’s method, suitable head expansions preserve membership. Girard’s reducibility candidates, in Proofs and Types, are closed under reduction, contain only terms all of whose runs end, and contain a neutral term, one that is not an abstraction, whenever they contain all its one-step reducts. A biorthogonal set is one equal to its double orthogonal: it contains every term that passes all the tests its members pass. The condition used here differs from each of these.

‘Runs to I’ is a type, since it shades exactly one fibre. No probe defines it, however, because it holds of I and not of S K K, and those two give the same readings under every probe. ‘Returns its argument’ is defined by the probe, the hole applied to x, with the reading x, and its shading is drawn in blue. A term without variables has it when the probe reads x from it, and a term with variables when the probe reads x from every closed instance. The figure, computed in the browser, applies the probe directly to each term, those with variables included. On terms without variables the predicate is a type, and it shades the fibres of I, S K K and S K S together, which are the fibres that sameness under probes merged. Steps settle which predicates are types. Probes are the usual way of defining one, and a type defined by a probe cannot separate terms without variables of their own that probes cannot separate.

The fundamental lemma of the first section said that every rule for building a term preserves membership in the semantic type. It can now be followed for a single atom. Let the semantic types A and B be types, predicates that do not leak. An application test for A to B to A takes a term u in the semantic type A and a term v in the semantic type B, puts the term under test in the probe, the hole applied to u and v, and asks whether the result is in the semantic type A. It plugs a term into a context as the probes of the table did, but its verdict is membership in a type and not a reading. A term t is in the semantic type A to B to A if it passes every such test, that is, if t u v is in the semantic type A whenever u is in the semantic type A and v is in the semantic type B. K passes every such test. To see why, take such a u and v. The term K u v steps to u, and u is in the semantic type A by assumption. Since the semantic type A does not leak, K u v is in it as well, and that is what the test required.

Section: The definition.

A presentation consists of descriptions built from atoms, steps between descriptions, and an observation. In general an observation pairs the descriptions with a collection of observers and gives each pair a verdict. A probe is one kind of observer, and the observation then says what probes there are and what kind of answer a probe returns. In this essay the probes have been contexts written in the calculus itself, and the answer has been a reading or none. The application tests of the section on types use the same contexts with a different verdict, membership in a type. This is one choice among several. The other entries in the table are derived from these three parts.

The table sets each term of the framework beside its counterpart in operational semantics. A description is a term, as text. An atom is a node of syntax: a constant, a variable, a term former. A step is one use of a rule of the small-step semantics. Computation is a run: a reduction sequence. Conversion is steps taken forwards or backwards; definitional equality. A fibre is a class of terms under conversion. An observation is a pairing of descriptions with observers; here, the probes that are available and the kind of answer they return. A probe is, here, a context with a hole, used on a term and run. A family is a set of evidence for each term and a map for each step; a predicate is one case. A behaviour is a family in which each step carries evidence across in one way; a type is the case where the evidence is a yes or a no. Closure is closing a predicate, as the control marked ‘Close it’ does in the figure of leaks.

A family gives each term a set of possible evidence, such as proofs that the term belongs to a type, and each step a map that carries evidence for the step’s start to evidence for its end. Carrying evidence along a run applies the maps of its steps in turn. A predicate is the family whose set has one element at a term that satisfies it and none elsewhere. In a behaviour the map of each step is invertible, so evidence can be carried across a step in exactly one way, in either direction. Subject reduction says that evidence can be carried forward across a step, and subject expansion that it can be carried back. A behaviour also asks that each step carry it in only one way. A note states this in the language of categories. Take the category whose objects are descriptions and whose maps are runs. A family is a functor from it to sets. A step from s to t gives a map of representable families, from the family represented by t to the family represented by s, and these maps generate an orthogonal factorization system on the category of families. A family is a behaviour exactly when its map to the terminal family has unique lifting against these maps, which is the condition in the text, and exactly when it sends every step to a bijection. That the factorization system exists follows from the small-generation argument of Freyd and Kelly, since the category of runs is small; it has not been mechanized. The points of the line, when it is drawn for sameness after running, are the connected components of the category. Closing a predicate is a reflector in these terms. Putting another category in the place of sets changes the kind of answer a probe returns.

Behaviours give the framework its name. A calculus may count two runs with the same start and finish as one. Keep those agreements and give each step a reverse that undoes it. This construction is called localization; a note cites Gabriel and Zisman. A family of sets on the runs made reversible in this way gives a behaviour on the original runs, by forgetting the reverses, and every behaviour comes from such a family in essentially one way. Localization semantics takes the terms, with their runs made reversible, as their meaning. The framework still keeps the original runs, with each step going from its start to its end.

The definition of a behaviour is about one step at a time. It leaves open whether two different runs between the same two terms carry evidence to the same place. For a predicate the question cannot arise, since a term either satisfies a predicate or does not, and there is nothing further to differ. For evidence with more content, two runs can carry it to different places.

Section: What a type cannot see.

A type was defined as a predicate that no step changes. It shades whole fibres, where a fibre is a set of terms joined by steps, and so it amounts to a set of points on the line. Whether a term belongs to it depends only on the term’s fibre, and not on anything inside the fibre. Inside a fibre, runs can differ in length, two runs between the same terms may or may not count as the same run, and the order of evaluation can decide whether a value is reached at all.

For length, K a bracket I b reaches a in one step or in two, as in the figure of fourteen terms. The cost of a run is here defined as the number of its steps. Whether a count of steps is a fair measure of time is a separate question. For the lambda calculus, Accattoli and Dal Lago prove that a leftmost-outermost run to normal form can be carried out on a machine in time polynomial in the size of the starting term and in the number of its beta-steps, with the normal form produced in a shared representation rather than written out in full. No such theorem is claimed here for the step count of combinatory logic. Whether two runs between the same terms are the same run is not settled by the steps. A calculus may declare that two such runs count as one, and then every behaviour has to respect the declaration, so that evidence carried along either run arrives in the same state. Section: When probes can read atoms.

So far a probe could read an outcome only if it was written in the probe’s own variables. Some calculi give a probe more. Jay’s tree calculus can inspect program structure. With the triage rules used here, a program can ask of any closed normal form whether it is a leaf, a stem or a fork, and take it apart accordingly, so that a probe can read atoms. A note cites Jay’s book and the tree calculus specification, and adds that Jay and Vergara, in ‘Confusion in the Church–Turing thesis’, compare calculi by a notion of computational equivalence that depends on how programs are encoded. The checkbox marked ‘Let probes read code’, under the table of six terms and three probes, gave a probe this power in combinatory logic. With it ticked, a probe reads the whole normal form after running, constants included. Figure 7 shows what it does to sameness under probes.

One probe tells S K leaf from S K K by the right branch of the top fork, and the triage probe puts I, S K K and S K bracket leaf K on three points. On closed terms, one of which has a value, the tree calculus’s own probes, which read only the variables they supply, give sameness after running: a probe can compare a closed term’s value with a given closed value shape by shape, by triage, and return a fresh variable on a match. This has been argued and not mechanized. Terms with variables are compared through their closed instances, and one replacement already separates a from b: with I for a and K for b, the probe, the hole applied to x and y, reads x y from one and x from the other. The points of the other terms in the figure are placed by fibre, as in the figure of three equalities.

With the box ticked, a probe distinguishes I, S K K and S K S by their text. For terms that have a value, sameness under probes then becomes sameness after running. It does not become sameness of text, since I a and a still share a point. Probes that read code are a different observation, with their own policy for variables: they read any normal form, the term’s own variables included, and so compare terms with variables directly rather than through closed instances. A probe that reads code reads the normal form its run reaches, and terms joined by steps reach the same normal forms in every context, so no such probe can tell them apart.

In tree calculus, S K leaf and S K K, written leaf bracket leaf bracket leaf leaf close bracket close bracket leaf, and leaf bracket leaf bracket leaf leaf close bracket close bracket bracket leaf leaf, both return whatever they are given. They are different normal forms with different right branches. A probe tells them apart by taking the right branch of the top fork and reading x if it is a leaf and y if it is a stem. In combinatory logic, by contrast, S K y and I agree under every probe, whatever y is. Two of the general results of this section hold in any calculus with probes as in Lean: it has a context that is a single hole, in which a term put there is the term itself, a context can be put inside another, its steps can be taken inside a context, and its readable outcomes take no step. Tree calculus is one. Both results compare terms directly, without closed instances, and on terms without variables that is the essay’s rule. If the calculus is confluent, terms joined by steps agree under every probe. If, in addition, a probe may read every normal form it reaches, then a term with a value and any other term agree under every such probe exactly when they are joined by steps.

With probes that read code, two normal forms are equal under probes only when they are the same tree, so values have a single equality, decided by comparing text. Any change to the normal form of a function can then be detected by some program, so a function cannot be replaced by a different normal form without the change being seen, and abstraction is lost.

In the lambda calculus a probe tests a function only by applying it to arguments and reading what it returns, as the probes of the table of six terms did with I, S K K and S K S, and sameness under probes is much coarser than sameness after running. The framework does not choose between the two settings. The observation is one of its three parts, and the checkbox changes it while the descriptions and the steps stay as they are. In tree calculus the triage rules let the calculus’s own contexts read the code of closed terms.

Section: Changing the ingredients.

A presentation has three ingredients, the descriptions, the steps and the observation, and one decision goes with them, which is how much of the resulting picture to keep. The calculus of this essay made its choices early: its steps run one way, its probes supply an argument and read back an answer, and the essay has mostly kept the line, on which each fibre shows as a single point. The definition given above requires none of these choices.

The essay calls each way of making the choices a notion of computation. (The word computation by itself still means a run.) The usual notion, that of Turing machines and the lambda calculus, is one of them. Its descriptions are finite. A Turing machine’s program is a finite table of moves, and a description is a configuration of that program, a tape with a head and a state. A step is one move. The steps are given by finitely many rules that run one way, and each rule is local, in that it inspects a bounded part of the description. A probe supplies a number, which fixes the starting configuration, and reads a number from the configuration where the run ends, or reads nothing if the run does not end. And only the line is kept. Its points group programs by the partial function from numbers to numbers that they compute, so that a program is identified with that function.

In the 1930s Church and Kleene related lambda-definability to recursiveness, and Turing introduced his machines and related them to lambda-definability. With the standard ways of writing numbers in each, these models compute the same functions from numbers to numbers. In the modern presentation, where a run may fail to end, they put the same points on the line: the partial recursive functions, that is, the functions that some Turing machine computes, undefined where the machine does not halt. The original papers stated their results for total functions and, in Turing’s first paper, for computable sequences. That much is a theorem about the models named. The Church–Turing thesis is a further claim, that effective calculability, what a person can compute by following rules, is exactly what these models compute. In such a comparison the points group programs, and a probe runs a program from the configuration that its input fixes. A calculus adds no point if a Turing machine can carry out each of its probes: the input is written into the starting configuration by an effective rule, a fixed effective strategy chooses each next step, whether a run has ended can be decided effectively, and an ended run is read back as at most one number by an effective rule. A calculus of this kind that reaches all of these points is called universal, and every universal calculus has exactly these points on its line. This agreement is why the essay takes the usual notion as its point of reference. A note gives the sources: Church’s paper of 1936, which proposes identifying effective calculability with recursiveness, or with lambda-definability; Kleene’s, which relates the two; and Turing’s ‘On computable numbers’, which argues that his machines capture what a person can compute by a rule, and his ‘Computability and lambda-definability’, which relates his machines to lambda-definability, using the result of Church and Kleene.

The agreement holds at one setting. The calculi that agree on the line still differ in their fibres, in what a run costs and in which runs count as the same. And the comparison needs an observation that belongs to none of the calculi compared. A Turing machine and a term of combinatory logic have no atoms and no steps in common. To compare them, the same number is written as marks on the tape and as a term, each is run, and the two answers are read back in the same way. That probe stands outside both calculi, and the thesis is a statement about this one choice of observation. A note adds that Boker and Dershowitz argue that a comparison of calculi depends on how data are written in each, as Jay and Vergara do for calculi that can read their own programs, and cites Felleisen for comparison by what can be expressed under specified translations.

Sameness under probes becomes sameness after running on terms that have a value. A program can then tell different normal forms apart by inspecting their code.

Card five. Observation: a probe may watch a run as it goes. Instances: concurrent processes, in which two runs from one state need not meet again. Two processes can show the same sequences of actions and still offer different next moves. The same steps support a range of equalities, from sameness of traces to bisimulation, one for each kind of observer.

Card six. Observation: the answer is a proof, and there may be several. Instances: dependent type theory, where a proof that two terms are equal is itself a term. Two proofs of one equality can in turn be equal or not. The proofs have steps and fibres of their own, and the picture is repeated one level up.

Card seven. Steps: steps are allowed in both directions. Instances: a group given by generators and relations; descriptions are words, and a step applies a relation. The steps single out no normal form and no order of evaluation. In place of a value a word names a group element. For a word that names the identity in the presented group, the fewest deletions of a relator or its inverse that take it to the empty word, where before each deletion the word may be replaced at no cost by any word equal to it in the free group, equals its area, the fewest conjugates of relators or their inverses whose product it equals in the free group. Whether two words share a point can be undecidable.

Card eight. What is kept: the fibres are kept as well as the line. Instances: associativity; the loop omega omega. Loops and holes appear, as in the pentagon of bracketings. Evidence carried round a loop can come back changed: on the bracketings, a behaviour that carries evidence backwards acts differently along the two routes.

The cards on observation can also be sorted by what they give up. The probes of this essay have four properties at once. A step in the term under test never changes a probe’s reading, and for terms with variables the same holds of each closed instance. A probe is made from the calculus’s own terms. A probe reads only what it supplied, and terms with variables of their own are compared through their closed instances. And the answer is a reading or none. In any calculus with probes as in Lean, confluence gives the implication from sameness after running to sameness under probes, compared directly, which on terms without variables is the essay’s rule. Probes made of terms compose: a context put in the hole of another is again a context, and putting a term in it is putting the term in the inner context and the result in the outer one. So in combinatory logic, sameness under probes through closed instances, the essay’s rule, is kept by every context, and in any calculus with probes as in Lean, sameness under probes compared directly is kept by every context. Reading only what was supplied is what lets probes be coarser than running. In combinatory logic they are, since I, S K K and S K S agree under every probe and lie in three fibres. In a confluent calculus with probes as in Lean, a probe that may read every normal form it reaches makes sameness under probes into sameness after running on terms that have a value. With a reading or none as the answer, a probe defines a predicate, and by the first property that predicate is a type. Types in general were defined by steps alone. Each card on observation drops one of them, or none.

The table of the written version gives, for each card, the change to the probes. A probe that returns a cost as well as an answer: a step can change the reported cost, so the probe sees which way steps go. A probe that may watch a run as it goes: a probe can see an action as it happens, and so which way steps go. A probe that may read code: a probe reads more than it supplied. The answer is a proof, and there may be several: the answer is no longer a reading. A probe that supplies a program, which may be called but not read: it keeps all four properties and accepts more probes. Numbers in and out, compared across calculi: the probe is made from neither calculus, as in the figure of doubling.

The steps go both ways, so they single out no normal form, and the picture has no input, output or order of evaluation. The three ingredients are present all the same, with the observer that counts letters as the observation. Cost is present too. The number of steps a run has taken is the cost of that run, and the least cost of a run between two words is the area between their paths. Each step moves the path across one square, and a step toward the other path removes one square from between them, so no run is shorter than that area and some run has exactly that length. With other relations the same questions become hard. Whether two words name one element is called the word problem of the group, and for some groups no procedure decides it.

The last card, on keeping the fibres, changes what is kept. The picture of running can be kept at three levels. At the first, each fibre is counted as one piece, and the pieces are the points of the line. At the second, the arrowheads are forgotten and a fibre is a graph. A loop in it is a round trip from a term back to itself, such as the pentagon of bracketings read by going down one route and back up the other. Some round trips shrink to nothing. A step followed by the same step taken back cancels, and an agreed equation between two runs lets either replace the other inside a round trip. With the equation that counts the pentagon’s two routes as one, its round trip becomes one route out and the same route back, which cancels. A hole is a round trip that cannot be shrunk to nothing in these ways, and without that equation the pentagon is one. (A hole in this sense is not the hole of a probe’s context.) At the third, the arrowheads are kept, and a normal form is the term at which the runs of a fibre end. Cost can be added to the graph or to the picture with direction, as the length of a route. A note cites Lawvere’s ‘Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories’ for length as a structure of the same kind as truth.

On a fibre without holes, keeping the graph or the direction tells a type nothing that the single point did not. A family attaches evidence to a term, a yes or a no for a predicate and a proof or a number for a richer family. On a fibre without holes, evidence carried along any two runs between the same terms arrives in the same state. A behaviour is the framework’s wider notion of type, in which each step carries evidence across in exactly one way. Over such a fibre it amounts to evidence attached to the value, and for a predicate to a yes or a no about the value. In a calculus whose rules do not overlap, such as combinatory logic, the fibre over a value is of this kind, provided that runs are counted as one by Lévy’s permutation equivalence. Two steps at separate places can be taken in either order. When a step discards an argument, a step taken inside that argument beforehand leaves nothing behind, so the two runs of K a bracket I b count as one. When a step copies an argument, a step taken inside it beforehand stands for the same step taken in each copy afterwards. Runs that differ in these ways count as one, and by Huet and Lévy’s normal derivation theorem any two runs from one term to its value then count as one. (The Lean development counts runs as one by a list of elementary squares, and that this list gives Lévy’s equivalence has not been mechanized.) The framework adds the step from there to the absence of holes, and it has been mechanized. Take the category whose maps are the runs of the fibre, so counted. If every term has exactly one run to one term, that term is terminal, and inverting every run leaves a single point: the fibre has no holes. A behaviour inverts every step, and so every run, and over such a category the behaviours are the same as sets, that is, as evidence about that term. Each step of a round trip, followed by the run from its end to that term, is a run to that term, and so it counts as one with the run from its start. The round trip can therefore be pulled onto that term a step at a time, and there it is empty. A note adds that this way of counting runs as one is due to J.-J. Lévy, in his thesis of 1978, and is treated in Huet and Lévy and in Terese, Term Rewriting Systems. Permutation equivalence accounts for steps that are erased or copied, not only for steps taken in another order. To see that any two runs from one term to its value are identified, project either run along the other, and nothing remains to do at the value. Van Oostrom and de Vrijer show four ways of counting runs as one to agree, with a remark that qualifies one of the correspondences for rules that collapse a term to one of its arguments, as the rules for I and K do. Among the pairs that permutation equivalence identifies are a run that discards an argument at once and a run that first takes a step inside that argument. In interaction nets no step discards or copies another, so two different steps from one net lead to a common net in one more step each, and every run that ends in an irreducible net has the same length, as Lafont shows. The calculus of bracketings, with its one rule, bracket x y close bracket z steps to x bracket y z, is not of this kind. Its rule can be used at two places in bracket bracket x y close bracket z close bracket w that share a bracket, neither use discards or copies the other, and the pentagon is the hole that this overlap leaves, which is why it needs an equation of its own.

Section: Why localization.

Three older ways of giving a calculus a meaning compare terms by how they run, by the values a model gives them, or by what probes read. They differ from localization semantics in what they keep. Operational semantics gives a term its steps and runs, and keeps how the term moves. Denotational semantics, with steps read as equalities in an ordered model, gives a term a value, the same for terms joined by steps, and keeps values that depend only on the fibre. Observational semantics gives a term its answers under every probe, and keeps agreement under probes. Localization semantics gives a term the term itself, with its runs made reversible, and keeps the points of the line, and the round trips within each fibre.

Operational semantics supplies the steps, and localization semantics takes them as given. A denotational semantics gives each term a value in a model. Consider a model in which values are ordered, and two values are equal if each is below the other, such as a domain ordered by information, or a set with equality as its order. Interpret each run by an inequality from its starting value to its ending value: the empty run gives equality, and joining two runs joins their inequalities. If each step gives an inequality that holds both ways, all the terms of a fibre, the terms joined to one another by steps taken either way, get the same value. Any two runs with the same start and finish give the same inequality, so a round trip reads the same as staying put. Such a semantics keeps at most the points of the line, one for each fibre. The two routes round the pentagon of bracketings still differ after the steps in that example are made reversible.

Observation is the third ingredient of a presentation, and it is kept apart from the steps. The probes compare terms in every context, which is the method of J. H. Morris. A note adds that Morris, in chapter III of his thesis, requires the two terms, put in any context, to have the same normal form whenever either has one; the probes here compare only the outcomes they are allowed to read. Keeping observation apart lets the probes change while the steps stay fixed, as in the section ‘When probes can read atoms’.

Squier theory asks a related question about string rewriting. Its routes may use steps in either direction, and it already counts the two orders of two independent steps as one route. It asks whether finitely many further equations between routes, usable inside any surrounding word, identify every two routes with the same ends; a note cites Squier, Otto and Kobayashi, and Guiraud and Malbos. Observers that see which way a step goes are left for a later essay.

Section: Summary.

Atoms are the parts from which a term is written. Probes are experiments on a term, and in this essay they read only what they supplied. Running lies between the two, and its steps join descriptions into fibres. Equality of terms can be taken as sameness of text, as membership of one fibre, or as agreement under every probe, and each of these is coarser than the one before.

A type is a predicate that no step changes. Probes give one way to define a type. In the fundamental lemma for K, since K u v steps to u, membership of u carries back to K u v, the term the application test asks about. Membership in a type depends only on a term’s fibre. A type does not record the length of a run, the difference between two runs, or a loop, whether or not some order of evaluation escapes it.

A presentation has three ingredients: the descriptions, the steps and the observation. The usual notion of computation is one way of choosing them, with finite and local rules that run one way, probes that deal in numbers, and only the line kept. At that setting every universal calculus, meaning one that computes all that a Turing machine can and whose probes a Turing machine can carry out, with a fixed effective order of steps and at most one number read back, has the same line, the partial recursive functions. Changing what a probe returns or may do, letting the steps run both ways, or keeping the fibres gives other notions. Keeping the fibres can distinguish runs that end at the same value, when the chosen equations between runs do not identify them. A note names three works that stand close to this one. B. P. Hilken, ‘Towards a proof theory of rewriting: the simply typed 2-lambda-calculus’, studies equations between rewrites in that calculus. M. Fiore and P. Saville, ‘Coherence and normalisation-by-evaluation for bicategorical cartesian closed structure’, prove that parallel structural 2-cells in a free cartesian closed bicategory coincide. J. C. Baez and C. Williams, ‘Enriched Lawvere theories for operational semantics’, obtain denotational semantics from operational semantics by a change of enriching category.
