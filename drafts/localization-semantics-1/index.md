> **Abstract.** A type can be given by rules for building a term or by tests applied to what the term does. This report develops the second view into a framework, localization semantics. A calculus is presented by descriptions built from atoms, steps between descriptions, and an observation: here, probes that run a term in a context and read back an answer. Terms joined by steps form fibres, and a type is a predicate that no step changes, so it holds of a whole fibre or of none of it. The examples are in combinatory logic, and selected figures also have tree-calculus or interaction-net views. The report compares three equalities on terms, shows what a type cannot see inside a fibre (how long a run is, whether two runs count as one, whether the order of evaluation reaches a value), and shows what changes when probes may read code. It ends by setting localization semantics beside operational, denotational and observational semantics. Many of its claims are mechanized in Lean, and the text says which, and where a claim is argued by hand.

Typing rules say how a program of a given type is built. A semantic type, which is a predicate on terms, tests what the program does when it is used. Proofs of type soundness go from the first to the second: a program built by the rules is safe to run, or passes the tests of its type, shown by syntactic means, as Wright and Felleisen do,[^sn1] or with types as predicates, as Milner does[^sn1b] and, at scale, Jung, Jourdan, Krebbers and Dreyer do.[^sn1c]

[^sn1]: For the syntactic method see A. K. Wright and M. Felleisen, [‘A syntactic approach to type soundness’](https://doi.org/10.1006/inco.1994.1093), *Information and Computation* 115(1), 1994, 38–94.
[^sn1b]: For types as predicates see R. Milner, [‘A theory of type polymorphism in programming’](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0000(78)90014-4), *Journal of Computer and System Sciences* 17(3), 1978, 348–375.
[^sn1c]: For a recent use of types as predicates at scale see R. Jung, J.-H. Jourdan, R. Krebbers and D. Dreyer, [‘RustBelt: securing the foundations of the Rust programming language’](https://doi.org/10.1145/3158154), *Proceedings of the ACM on Programming Languages* 2(POPL), 2018, article 66.

This essay explains a framework, localization semantics, that separates how a term is written from what it does when used, as the two accounts of typing do for typed programs. Atoms make up the text of a term, and probes test what the term does. In full these are description atoms and behaviour probes. The distinction is easy to miss, because a probe is itself written with atoms, and the two look alike on the page. Steps run a term, and they sit between the two. The framework calls running computation.

The distinction bears on choices that a language designer makes. It shows which equalities a type checker can decide by running terms and which have to be proved. It shows how an operator that inspects code tells apart terms that application treats alike, which costs abstraction. It names what a type does not record, such as the cost of a program, and it places the usual notion of computation among several.

Each ingredient keeps one colour through all the figures. Ochre marks descriptions, the atoms and the text made from them. Green marks steps and runs. Blue marks observation, which covers probes, what they read, and evidence. Black marks what is kept, the line and its points. Each point stands for a group of terms counted as equal, and the line sets the groups side by side. Red marks a leak or a clash. A filled green button starts a run.

**Combinatory logic** is the calculus of the examples. Its terms are built by application from the constants `S`, `K` and `I` and from variables, and its three rules are given in the section on atoms and probes.

**Tree calculus** has one constant, the leaf `△`, and five rules. The K-rule is `△ △ y z → y` and the S-rule is `△ (△ x) y z → x z (y z)`. Three rules of triage choose by the shape of the third argument:

```text
△ (△ w x) y △ → w
△ (△ w x) y (△ u) → x u
△ (△ w x) y (△ u v) → y u v
```
These are the rules of the tree calculus [specification](https://treecalcul.us/specification/), where they are called triage calculus. J. Bader suggested them as an alternative to the original rules of B. Jay.[^jay21] A term with no redex anywhere is a normal form. Every closed normal form is a leaf, a stem `△ u` or a fork `△ u v`, with `u` and `v` normal forms as well. With variables there are other normal forms, such as `a` and `a △`. [Tree reduction is confluent](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Tree/Calculus.lean#L348 "Mechanized: Tree.calculus_confluent").[^proofs] The figures write `K = △△`, `I = △(△K)△`, `S x y = △(△x)y` and `ω = S I I`, so that the terms read as in combinatory logic.

[^jay21]: B. Jay, *Reflective Programs in Tree Calculus*, 2021.
[^proofs]: A statement with a dashed underline links to its machine-checked proof in Lean. A name with one, such as the eager table, links to its definition in Lean.

**Interaction nets** are due to Y. Lafont.[^lafont90] [The eager table](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/tree-semantics/blob/5147cfad47f152f30dac95f8344105c867561b5c/TreeCalculus/IxTree.lean#L128 "Mechanized in tree-semantics: Ix.TC.eagerTable") of tree-semantics compiles a term to a net of agents. The table has fifteen rules, and its agents stand for the leaf, the stem and the fork, and for applying, triage, copying and erasing. For closed trees, tree-semantics proves that [if a tree applied to another evaluates strictly to a value, the compiled net has a run to an irreducible net that reads back that value](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/tree-semantics/blob/5147cfad47f152f30dac95f8344105c867561b5c/TreeCalculus/IxTreeEager.lean#L87 "Mechanized in tree-semantics: Ix.TC.eager_sound"). A step is one interaction, between two agents joined at their principal ports. The table is eager, so a net runs an argument even when that argument will be discarded. In the figure of running, under ‘One step’, the thick green wire joins the two agents about to interact, and the label ‘result’ marks the result port. The figures name each net by the term read back at its result port, and a variable that a probe supplies appears as a free wire. The browser’s model compiles and reads back such open terms, and the proofs do not cover them.

## Two accounts of a typed program

A type can be given by rules for building a term, or by tests applied to a finished one. The rest of the essay uses the two judgments below and the set $\llbracket A \rrbracket$. How a derivation is built matters less here, and readers who do not know typing judgments can pass over those details.

### Syntactic typing

$$
\Gamma \vdash t : A
$$

Read: under the assumptions $\Gamma$, the term `t` has type `A`. A derivation proves the statement. It is built from rules, one for each way of forming a term, and arguments about it go by induction on the derivation.

### Semantic typing

$$
t \in \llbracket A \rrbracket
$$

Read: `t` belongs to the set of terms that pass the tests for `A`, a predicate on terms. A term belongs to $\llbracket A \to B \rrbracket$ if applying it to any member of $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ yields a member of $\llbracket B \rrbracket$.

The two are connected by the fundamental lemma of logical relations, which states that every typing rule preserves membership in the semantic type. A closed term that can be derived therefore passes every test that its type imposes. Under assumptions $\Gamma$ the same holds once each variable is replaced by a term that passes the tests of its assumed type, as in the work of Tait,[^sn2] Plotkin[^sn2b] and Statman.[^sn2c]

[^sn2]: W. W. Tait, [‘Intensional interpretations of functionals of finite type I’](https://doi.org/10.2307/2271658), *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 32(2), 1967, 198–212.
[^sn2b]: G. D. Plotkin, [‘Lambda-definability and logical relations’](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/gdp/publications/logical_relations_1973.pdf), Memorandum SAI-RM-4, University of Edinburgh, 1973 (the link is to the author’s copy).
[^sn2c]: R. Statman, [‘Logical relations and the typed λ-calculus’](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0019-9958(85)80001-2), *Information and Control* 65(2–3), 1985, 85–97.

The framework applies the same division to terms. A term considered as a piece of text is called a *description*. It is the tree obtained by grouping the term’s symbols, as a parser does, and two descriptions are equal only if they are the same tree. Descriptions are made of atoms. What a term does when it is used is a separate matter, and probes find it out.

## Atoms and probes

The examples are written in combinatory logic, introduced by Schönfinkel[^sn3] and developed systematically by Curry and Feys.[^sn3b] The version used here has three constants and no binding of variables. Two terms written side by side form an application of the first to the second, and applications group to the left, so that `S K K a` means `((S K) K) a`. The constants are defined by three rules: `I x → x`, `K x y → x` and `S x y z → x z (y z)`. In a functional language `I` is the identity function and `K` is `const`, and `S` passes one argument to two functions and applies the first result to the second. A term is run by using the rules until none applies. In the rules, `x`, `y` and `z` stand for any terms. In the terms themselves the letters `a`, `b`, `x` and `y` are variables, which have no rules of their own, and `ω` abbreviates `S I I`.

[^sn3]: M. Schönfinkel, [‘Über die Bausteine der mathematischen Logik’](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01448013), *Mathematische Annalen* 92(3–4), 1924, 305–316.
[^sn3b]: H. B. Curry and R. Feys, *Combinatory Logic*, vol. I, North-Holland, Amsterdam, 1958.

An *atom* is a node of syntax. Here the atoms are the constants, the variables, and application. A term is a tree of atoms, and to ask about its atoms is to ask what the text consists of. A programmer would call this the white-box view.

A *probe* is an experiment on a term, the black-box view. The probe supplies a context with a hole, such as `[ ] x y`, whose variables are fresh, new names that occur nowhere in the term under test. A probe is run only on a term without variables of its own, such as each of the six terms of the figure, so every variable in an outcome came from the probe. The term is put in the hole and the result is run. The probe then reads the outcome, but it may read only what it supplied. If the outcome is written entirely in the probe’s own variables, as `x y` is, the probe reads it. If the outcome still contains `S`, `K` or `I`, those symbols are part of the term’s text, and the probe may not look at them. It can try another context instead, for instance one that supplies another argument. Under `[ ] x`, the term `I` gives `x`, which the probe reads. The term `K I` gives `I`, which still contains a constant, so the probe does not read it; under `[ ] x y` the same term gives `y`, which it reads. When the outcome cannot be read, the figure says ‘not read’, and a run that never reaches a normal form gives no reading either. A term with variables of its own, such as `K a`, is not put in a probe directly. It is compared through its closed instances, the terms obtained by replacing each of its variables by a term without variables. A variable stands for any term, so two such terms are the same under probes when every replacement of their variables by terms without variables, made in both at once, gives two terms that no probe tells apart. Two simpler rules fail. If a probe could read the term’s own variables, [`a I` and `a K` would be the same under probes, since neither can ever be read, yet they come apart once `I` replaces `a`](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L388 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEq_not_subst_stable"). If instead a probe were run on the term itself, with every outcome containing those variables unreadable, it would have to discard `a` before reading anything, and `a` would look like a term with no value. For terms with no variables of their own, such as these six, [comparing their closed instances is the same as reading them directly](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L131 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEqCl_iff_of_closed"), and the stricter rule gives the same readings, since such a term puts no variable of its own into an outcome. This restriction is a decision about observation. It does not follow from the three rules, and it can be changed while they stay as they are. The probes follow Morris in comparing terms in contexts;[^sn4] here only outcomes written in the probe’s own variables are readable.

[^sn4]: The definition follows J. H. Morris, [*Lambda-Calculus Models of Programming Languages*](https://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/149376), PhD thesis, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, submitted 1968 and issued as a Project MAC technical report in December 1968 (the link is to the report edition; MIT’s thesis catalogue dates the degree 1969), which compares terms by placing them in contexts. Here the observation is a reading built only from the context’s own variables, a variant suited to a calculus with free variables, and terms with variables are compared through their closed instances.

Two terms without variables are the same under every probe if no context, however elaborate and however many times it uses the term, leads to different readings. Two terms with variables are the same if this holds of the pair of closed instances given by each replacement of their variables. The figure tries three simple contexts on six terms. For these six terms, [agreement in the figure implies agreement under every probe](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L349 "Mechanized: SKI.six_terms").

In interaction nets, the small green number in a cell is run data, the number of interactions the browser’s reducer took. It is not part of the reading, and the points ignore it. A probe that also returned the count would tell `K I` from `K (S K K)`.

**In tree calculus** the table uses `S K (△K)` in place of `S K S` and adds a fourth probe, the triage probe. Write `triage(a, b, c)` for `△(△ a b) c`. The triage probe has the following form.

```text
triage(x, triage(y, K z, △), △) (triage(K △, K (K △), K I) [ ])
```
Its inner part takes the right branch of the term’s top fork. Its outer part returns *x* for a leaf, *y* for a stem of a leaf, and *z* for a stem of a stem. For example `I` is `△(△K)△`, whose right branch is the leaf `△`, so the probe reads *x* from it. [The table’s readings in all four columns are as shown](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Tree/Essay.lean#L101 "Mechanized: Tree.tree_six_terms"). The triage probe therefore separates the three identities, whose counterparts in combinatory logic, [`I`, `S K K` and `S K S`, agree under every probe](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L349 "Mechanized: SKI.six_terms"). The table still groups `K I` and `K (S K K)`. [The column `[ ] x y` separates the three groups, the triage probe separates the identities within theirs](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Tree/Essay.lean#L101 "Mechanized: Tree.tree_six_terms"), and the triage probe used after `[ ] x` reads *x* from `K I` and *y* from `K (S K K)`. So in tree calculus [the probes separate the three identities pairwise, separate `K I` from `K (S K K)`, and separate the representatives `I`, `K I` and `K` of the three groups pairwise](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Tree/Essay.lean#L161 "Mechanized: Tree.tree_six_distinct"). Each group shares its reading under `[ ] x y`, and the three groups’ readings differ, so no two of the six terms are the same under probes.

**In interaction nets** the browser compiles each experiment to a net and runs it by interactions, and the readings are those of tree calculus. Each cell also shows, as a small number, how many interactions its run took in the browser’s reducer. These cells contain free wires, and their readings and counts are the browser’s and are not proved. Each count is of one run, in one order. In a well-formed net, under a well-formed table with at most one rule for each pair of agents, [two interactions either give the same net or lead to a common net in one more interaction each](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/tree-semantics/blob/5147cfad47f152f30dac95f8344105c867561b5c/TreeCalculus/IxConfluence.lean#L278 "Mechanized in tree-semantics: Ix.strong_confluence"). When two different nets result, this is a diamond. In general, [if a term that steps to two different terms always has each of them step, in one step, to a common term, then any two runs from one term that end in normal forms have the same length and end at the same normal form](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Diamond.lean#L90 "Mechanized: Diamond.equal_lengths"). So, as Lafont observed,[^lafont97] [under those conditions any two runs from the same net to irreducible nets take the same number of interactions](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/tree-semantics/blob/5147cfad47f152f30dac95f8344105c867561b5c/TreeCalculus/IxConfluence.lean#L308 "Mechanized in tree-semantics: Ix.Steps.equal_lengths"). The browser’s open nets are not covered by that proof. The count is not part of the reading, and the points group the rows by their readings alone. The card on cost, near the end of the essay, lets a probe return the count as well. Selecting a cell shows the term at the result port as the interactions go, with the number done so far in brackets.

[^lafont97]: Y. Lafont, [‘Interaction combinators’](https://doi.org/10.1006/inco.1997.2643), *Information and Computation* 137(1), 1997, 69–101, proposition 1 and the paragraph after it.

The figure shows two groups of rows that agree. The three identities, `I`, `S K K` and `S K S`, each return their argument, since `I x`, `S K K x` and `S K S x` all run to `x`. However a context copies or discards such a term, each copy that is applied returns what it is given, whichever identity it is. A copy left unapplied in the outcome puts a constant there, and then the probe may not read the outcome at all. So the three give the same reading in every context. `K I` and `K (S K K)` agree for the same reason, since each discards its first argument and leaves an identity.

Application is the plainest case of a symbol with two roles. Read as text, `S K K x` is a tree with an application node at its root, and that node is an atom like any other. Read as an experiment, the same expression is the probe `[ ] x` used on `S K K`.

The checkbox lifts the restriction. With it ticked, a probe runs as before and may then read the whole normal form it reaches, constants included, and the six rows separate.

|  | Atom | Probe |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A node of syntax | A context with a hole, and a rule for what may be read |
| Question | What does the text consist of? | What does the term do when used? |
| Equality | The same tree | The same readings under every probe |
| Method | Induction on the text | Extensionality: terms are equal if every use of them gives equal results |

Type theory already has names for the atoms that do each job. An introduction form is an atom that builds a value of a type, and an elimination form is an atom that builds a probe for one. A pair is built by pairing and tested by asking for either component. A function is built by abstraction, written `λ`, and tested by applying it to an argument, so application is an atom of the text and also the way a probe uses a term.[^sn5]

[^sn5]: In a functional language the constructors and `λ` are introduction forms, and application, projection and pattern matching are elimination forms. A positive type, such as a sum or the natural numbers, is determined by its introduction forms. A negative type, such as a function or a record, is determined by its eliminations. J.-M. Andreoli, [‘Logic programming with focusing proofs in linear logic’](https://doi.org/10.1093/logcom/2.3.297), *Journal of Logic and Computation* 2(3), 1992, 297–347; P. B. Levy, [*Call-by-Push-Value: A Functional/Imperative Synthesis*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-007-0954-6), Springer, 2003; N. Zeilberger, [*The Logical Basis of Evaluation Order and Pattern-Matching*](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~noam/thesis.pdf), PhD thesis, Carnegie Mellon University, 2009, technical report CMU-CS-09-122 (the link is to the author’s copy). For a language in which negative types are defined by their observations see A. Abel, B. Pientka, D. Thibodeau and A. Setzer, [‘Copatterns: programming infinite structures by observations’](https://doi.org/10.1145/2429069.2429075), *POPL* 2013, 27–38.

## Running a term

Between writing a term and probing it comes running it. A part of a term that matches the left side of a rule is a *redex*. A *step* replaces one redex by the right side of its rule, and a *run*, which the framework calls *computation*, is a sequence of steps. A term with no redex is a *normal form*, and when a run ends in a normal form, that normal form is a *value* of the term the run began with.[^sn6]

[^sn6]: For a small-step presentation, structural operational semantics, see G. D. Plotkin, [‘A structural approach to operational semantics’](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jlap.2004.05.001), Report DAIMI FN-19, Aarhus University, 1981, reprinted in *Journal of Logic and Algebraic Programming* 60–61, 2004, 17–139, and for a big-step presentation, natural semantics, G. Kahn, [‘Natural semantics’](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0039592), in *STACS 87*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 247, Springer, 1987, 22–39.

A term can have more than one redex, and so more than one run. The term `K a (I b)` has two runs that go all the way to a normal form. The K-rule can be used at once, which discards `I b` and gives `a` in one step. Or `I b` can be run first, which gives `K a b` and then `a`, in two steps. The term `a` is a normal form, because a variable has no rule, and so it is the value of both runs. In combinatory logic [any two finite runs from the same term can be continued until they meet](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Confluence.lean#L160 "Mechanized: SKI.confluent"), a property called confluence (see Huet and Lévy).[^sn7] A term therefore has at most one value, though its runs may differ in length.

[^sn7]: G. Huet and J.-J. Lévy, ‘Computations in orthogonal rewriting systems, I’ and ‘II’, in J.-L. Lassez and G. D. Plotkin (eds), *Computational Logic: Essays in Honor of Alan Robinson*, MIT Press, 1991, 395–414 and 415–443, ISBN 9780262121569.

**In tree calculus** the third identity is `S K (△K)`, and probes can triage. The triage probe of the first figure reads *x*, *y* and *z* from `I`, `S K K` and `S K (△K)`, so sameness under probes keeps the three apart. The three are named by what decides them, and each name keeps its colour for the rest of the essay. Sameness of text, the equality that atoms give, identifies two descriptions only when they are the same tree. Sameness after running identifies descriptions that lie in one fibre, joined to one another by steps, and it is the only one of the three defined by running. In a confluent calculus in which every term has a value, two terms lie in one fibre exactly when they run to the same value, so this equality can be decided by running both sides. An equality decided by running in this way is one source of what a proof assistant calls definitional equality.[^sn10b] Sameness under probes identifies descriptions that no probe can tell apart, and it is a form of what is usually called contextual or observational equivalence. Morris[^sn10] and Plotkin[^sn10c] give other instances of the contextual test.

[^sn10]: Morris, *Lambda-Calculus Models of Programming Languages*, cited above.
[^sn10c]: G. D. Plotkin, [‘LCF considered as a programming language’](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(77)90044-5), *Theoretical Computer Science* 5(3), 1977, 223–255.
[^sn10b]: For definitional equality in one proof assistant, as reduction together with η and further rules such as proof irrelevance, see [‘The type system’](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/The-Type-System/) in the Lean language reference.

Terms joined by steps agree under every probe, so [sameness after running implies sameness under probes](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L127 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEqCl_of_conv"). The reason is that a step inside a term is still a step when the term sits in a context. So if `t` steps to `u`, a probe’s run on `t` can begin by taking that step in each copy of `t` in the context, and continue as a run on `u`. A step is also still a step when the same terms replace the variables on both sides, so for terms with variables the argument applies to each pair of closed instances. Confluence is the property that two runs from one term can always be continued until they meet. By it, if the experiment on either term has a finite run to a normal form the probe can read, the experiment on the other has a run to that same normal form, and the probe reads the same thing from both.

The converse fails, and the terms `I`, `S K K` and `S K S` show it. In the table of six terms and three probes, in the section on atoms and probes, the three had the same readings in every column, so sameness under probes puts them on one point. Each is also a normal form already, and by confluence two different normal forms never lie in one fibre, so [sameness after running keeps them apart](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Confluence.lean#L240 "Mechanized: SKI.three_fibres"). The other points under probes are placed by fibre. The fibre of `a` stands apart from the loop because [a closed instance separates them](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L239 "Mechanized: SKI.not_probeEqCl_var_loop"): with `I` in place of `a`, the probe `[ ] x` reads `x` from it and nothing from the loop.

Where sameness under probes holds and running does not show it, the equality has to be proved. With addition defined by recursion on its first argument, `0 + x` steps to `x`, but `x + 0` does not step at all, because the recursion is waiting on the variable `x`. The two sides of `x + 0 = x` agree under every probe, yet no step joins them. In a proof assistant such an equation is stated as a proposition, an equality type, and here it has to be proved, by induction on `x`. An equality stated in this way is called propositional. A compiler’s optimiser works with sameness under probes from the other side. It may replace a term by any other over the same point, and it looks for one with a shorter run.

A rule can be added to bring sameness after running closer to sameness under probes. In combinatory logic, tick the box in the figure. The rule `S K y → I` is sound: for every `y` without variables, [`S K y` and `I` agree under every probe](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L330 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEq_SK"), and so for every `y`, since each closed instance of `S K y` is `S K` applied to a term without variables. Applied to an argument, [`S K y x` runs to `x` in two steps, whatever `y` is](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L93 "Mechanized: SKI.skRun"). With the rule added, `S K K` and `S K S` step to `I`, and their three separate points become one, as they were under probes. The argument given for the three identities in the section on atoms and probes applies to `S K y` as it does to them. In tree calculus the same rule is unsound, as the note under the figure explains.

The η-rule of the lambda calculus is a rule of this kind. In the lambda calculus `λx. t` is the function that takes an argument `x` and returns `t`, and combinatory logic can be read as the lambda calculus with these bound variables compiled away. The η-rule makes a function `f` interchangeable with `λx. f x`, the function that takes a fresh `x` and returns `f` applied to `x`. It says that a function is no more than what it returns when applied, which is the black-box view of the section on atoms and probes.[^sn11] When the calculus gives the terms of a type such a rule, a checker decides by running what would otherwise need a proof. Function and record types, which are known by their probes, have one in Lean and in Agda. Sums and the natural numbers have laws of the same kind, which Lean does not use as steps and leaves to be proved.[^sn12]

Schönfinkel, cited above, introduced the combinators in order to remove bound variables.

## Types

Any property of terms is a predicate on descriptions, and it can be drawn as shading on the boxes of the figure of fourteen terms. The property of mentioning `K` is such a predicate. It would make a poor type, because `K a (K a)` has the property and the term `a`, to which it steps, does not. A program would belong to the type before a step and not after it. A predicate of this kind *leaks*.

In this essay a *type* is a predicate that does not leak, so that no step leads from a term that satisfies it to a term that does not, in either direction. A fibre, as before, is a set of terms joined to one another by steps, and [a predicate that does not leak shades each fibre entirely or not at all](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L35 "Mechanized: SKI.IsType.conv"). It can therefore be stated by naming points on the line, when the line is drawn for sameness after running and has one point for each fibre. The framework’s word is *behaviour*, which is slightly wider. A behaviour may attach evidence to a term, and not only a yes or a no, and [a predicate is a behaviour exactly when it does not leak](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Predicates.lean#L33 "Mechanized: beh_iff_of_subsingleton_co"). Where the evidence is a yes or a no, the two notions coincide.

The accounts of types as predicates cited in the note each have a condition of this sort, and the conditions differ. Some require every run of a member to end. The term `K a (ω ω)`, where `ω ω` is the loop of the section on running, can step straight to `a` or run its argument for ever, so it fails the condition that every run end, while `a`, which is one step away, meets it. The types here are closed under steps in both directions, and the fundamental lemma below uses closure under steps taken backwards.[^sn13]

[^sn13]: In a standard saturated-set formulation of Tait’s method, suitable head expansions preserve membership. Girard’s reducibility candidates, in chapter 14 of the book cited below, are closed under reduction, contain only terms all of whose runs end, and contain a neutral term, one that is not an abstraction, whenever they contain all its one-step reducts. A biorthogonal set is one equal to its double orthogonal: it contains every term that passes all the tests its members pass. The condition used here differs from each of these. Tait, cited above; J.-Y. Girard, Y. Lafont and P. Taylor, *Proofs and Types*, Cambridge Tracts in Theoretical Computer Science 7, Cambridge University Press, 1989, ISBN 9780521371810, chapters 6 and 14; A. M. Pitts, [‘Parametric polymorphism and operational equivalence’](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0960129500003066), *Mathematical Structures in Computer Science* 10(3), 2000, 321–359; J. Vouillon and P.-A. Melliès, [‘Semantic types: a fresh look at the ideal model for types’](https://doi.org/10.1145/964001.964006), *POPL* 2004, 52–63.

**In tree calculus** the predicates are the same, over the tree terms. ‘Runs to `I`’ can now be defined by a probe as well, by an argument that has not been mechanized. [For closed trees under strict evaluation, equality is decided by a program](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/tree-semantics/blob/5147cfad47f152f30dac95f8344105c867561b5c/TreeCalculus/Programs.lean#L256 "Mechanized in tree-semantics: Tree.exists_eq"), a tree that compares two trees. On a match the probe returns a fresh variable that it supplied, which it may read. If there is no value, or no match, it has nothing to read. This defines the predicate on terms without variables. It extends the shading across every step it meets, in both directions, until nothing more can be added.

[‘Runs to `I`’ is a type](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L379 "Mechanized: SKI.isType_runsToI"), since it shades exactly one fibre. [No probe defines it](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L386 "Mechanized: SKI.runsToI_not_probe"), however, because it holds of `I` and not of `S K K`, and those two give the same readings under every probe. ‘Returns its argument’ is defined by the probe `[ ] x` with the reading `x`, and its shading is drawn in blue. A term without variables has it when the probe reads `x` from it, and a term with variables when the probe reads `x` from every closed instance. The figure, computed in the browser, applies the probe directly to each term, those with variables included. On the terms drawn this gives the same verdict as testing every closed instance, by an argument that has not been mechanized: a term that runs to `I` still does once its variables are replaced, and under the probe a term that runs to `a` gives `K x`, which is not read, once `K` replaces `a`. On terms without variables [the predicate is a type](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L370 "Mechanized: SKI.isType_probePred"), and it shades the fibres of `I`, `S K K` and `S K S` together, which are the fibres that sameness under probes merged. Steps settle which predicates are types. Probes are the usual way of defining one, and [a type defined by a probe cannot separate terms without variables of their own that probes cannot separate](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L375 "Mechanized: SKI.probePred_respects").

The fundamental lemma of the first section said that every rule for building a term preserves membership in the semantic type. It can now be followed for a single atom. Let $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ and $\llbracket B \rrbracket$ be types, predicates that do not leak. An application test for `A → B → A` takes a term `u` in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ and a term `v` in $\llbracket B \rrbracket$, puts the term under test in the probe `[ ] u v`, and asks whether the result is in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$. It plugs a term into a context as the probes of the table did, but its verdict is membership in a type and not a reading. A term `t` is in $\llbracket A \to B \to A \rrbracket$ if it passes every such test, that is, if `t u v` is in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ whenever `u` is in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ and `v` is in $\llbracket B \rrbracket$. [`K` passes every such test](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L78 "Mechanized: SKI.fundamental_K"). To see why, take such a `u` and `v`. The term `K u v` steps to `u`, and `u` is in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ by assumption. Since $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ does not leak, `K u v` is in $\llbracket A \rrbracket$ as well, and that is what the test required.

## The definition

A *presentation* consists of descriptions built from atoms, steps between descriptions, and an observation. In general an observation pairs the descriptions with a collection of observers and gives each pair a verdict. A probe is one kind of observer, and the observation then says what probes there are and what kind of answer a probe returns. In this essay the probes have been contexts written in the calculus itself, and the answer has been a reading or none. The application tests of the section on types use the same contexts with a different verdict, membership in a type. This is one choice among several. The other entries in the table are derived from these three parts.

| Framework | Operational semantics |
|---|---|
| Description | A term, as text |
| Atom | A node of syntax: a constant, a variable, a term former |
| Step | One use of a rule of the small-step semantics |
| Computation | A run: a reduction sequence |
| Conversion | Steps taken forwards or backwards; definitional equality |
| Fibre | A class of terms under conversion |
| Observation | A pairing of descriptions with observers; here, the probes that are available and the kind of answer they return |
| Probe | Here, a context with a hole, used on a term and run |
| Family | A set of evidence for each term and a map for each step; a predicate is one case |
| Behaviour | A family in which each step carries evidence across in one way. A type is the case where the evidence is a yes or a no. |
| Closure | Closing a predicate, as the control marked ‘Close it’ does in the figure of leaks |

A family gives each term a set of possible evidence, such as proofs that the term belongs to a type, and each step a map that carries evidence for the step’s start to evidence for its end. Carrying evidence along a run applies the maps of its steps in turn. A predicate is the family whose set has one element at a term that satisfies it and none elsewhere. In a behaviour [the map of each step is invertible](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L210 "Mechanized: beh_iff_map_bijective_co"), so evidence can be carried across a step in exactly one way, in either direction. Subject reduction says that evidence can be carried forward across a step, and subject expansion that it can be carried back. A behaviour also asks that each step carry it in only one way.[^sn14]

[^sn14]: Take the category whose objects are descriptions and whose maps are runs. A family is a functor from it to sets. A step $s \to t$ gives a map of representable families $\mathrm{Hom}(t, -) \to \mathrm{Hom}(s, -)$, and these maps generate an orthogonal factorization system on the category of families. [A family is a behaviour exactly when its map to the terminal family has unique lifting against these maps](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Presentation.lean#L57 "Mechanized: Presentation.beh_iff_M"), which is the condition in the text, and [exactly when it sends every step to a bijection](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L210 "Mechanized: beh_iff_map_bijective_co"). That the factorization system exists follows from the small-generation argument of Freyd and Kelly, since the category of runs is small; it has not been mechanized. The points of the line, when it is drawn for sameness after running, are the connected components of the category. Closing a predicate is a reflector in these terms. Putting another category in the place of sets changes the kind of answer a probe returns. See P. Freyd and G. M. Kelly, [‘Categories of continuous functors, I’](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-4049(72)90001-1), *Journal of Pure and Applied Algebra* 2(3), 1972, 169–191, and C. Cassidy, M. Hébert and G. M. Kelly, [‘Reflective subcategories, localizations and factorization systems’](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788700023624), *Journal of the Australian Mathematical Society (Series A)* 38(3), 1985, 287–329.

Behaviours give the framework its name. A calculus may count two runs with the same start and finish as one. Keep those agreements and give each step a reverse that undoes it. This construction, studied by Gabriel and Zisman,[^sn25] is called *localization*. [A family of sets on the runs made reversible in this way gives a behaviour on the original runs, by forgetting the reverses, and every behaviour comes from such a family in essentially one way](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L249 "Mechanized: behEquivLocalSystemsCo"). Localization semantics takes the terms, with their runs made reversible, as their meaning. The framework still keeps the original runs, with each step going from its start to its end.

[^sn25]: See P. Gabriel and M. Zisman, [*Calculus of Fractions and Homotopy Theory*](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-85844-4), Springer, 1967, chapter I.

The definition of a behaviour is about one step at a time. It leaves open whether two different runs between the same two terms carry evidence to the same place. For a predicate the question cannot arise, since a term either satisfies a predicate or does not, and there is nothing further to differ. For evidence with more content, two runs can carry it to different places.

## What a type cannot see

A type was defined as a predicate that no step changes. It shades whole fibres, where a fibre is a set of terms joined by steps, and so it amounts to a set of points on the line. Whether a term belongs to it depends only on the term’s fibre, and not on anything inside the fibre. Inside a fibre, runs can differ in length, two runs between the same terms may or may not count as the same run, and the order of evaluation can decide whether a value is reached at all.

For length, `K a (I b)` reaches `a` in one step or in two, as in the figure of fourteen terms. The cost of a run is here defined as the number of its steps. Whether a count of steps is a fair measure of time is a separate question, which van Emde Boas treats for machine models.[^sn15] For the λ-calculus, Accattoli and Dal Lago prove that a leftmost-outermost run to normal form can be carried out on a machine in time polynomial in the size of the starting term and in the number of its β-steps, with the normal form produced in a shared representation rather than written out in full.[^sn15b] No such theorem is claimed here for the step count of combinatory logic. Whether two runs between the same terms are the same run is not settled by the steps. A calculus may declare that two such runs count as one, and then every behaviour has to respect the declaration, so that evidence carried along either run arrives in the same state. ## When probes can read atoms

So far a probe could read an outcome only if it was written in the probe’s own variables. Some calculi give a probe more. Jay’s tree calculus can inspect program structure.[^sn17] With the triage rules used here, a program can ask of any closed normal form whether it is a leaf, a stem or a fork, and take it apart accordingly, so that a probe can read atoms. The checkbox marked ‘Let probes read code’, under the table of six terms and three probes, gave a probe this power in combinatory logic. With it ticked, a probe reads the whole normal form after running, constants included. The figure below shows what it does to sameness under probes.

Tree calculus is one. Both results compare terms directly, without closed instances, and on terms without variables that is the essay’s rule. If the calculus is confluent, [terms joined by steps agree under every probe](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Calculus.lean#L154 "Mechanized: Calculus.probeEq_of_conv"). With probes that read code, two normal forms are equal under probes only when they are the same tree, so values have a single equality, decided by comparing text. Any change to the normal form of a function can then be detected by some program, so a function cannot be replaced by a different normal form without the change being seen, and abstraction is lost.

In the lambda calculus a probe tests a function only by applying it to arguments and reading what it returns, as the probes of the table of six terms did with `I`, `S K K` and `S K S`, and sameness under probes is much coarser than sameness after running. The framework does not choose between the two settings. The observation is one of its three parts, and the checkbox changes it while the descriptions and the steps stay as they are. In tree calculus the triage rules let the calculus’s own contexts read the code of closed terms.

## Changing the ingredients

A presentation has three ingredients, the descriptions, the steps and the observation, and one decision goes with them, which is how much of the resulting picture to keep. The calculus of this essay made its choices early: its steps run one way, its probes supply an argument and read back an answer, and the essay has mostly kept the line, on which each fibre shows as a single point. The definition given above requires none of these choices.

The essay calls each way of making the choices a notion of computation. (The word computation by itself still means a run.) The usual notion, that of Turing machines and the lambda calculus, is one of them. Its descriptions are finite. A Turing machine’s program is a finite table of moves, and a description is a configuration of that program, a tape with a head and a state. A step is one move. The steps are given by finitely many rules that run one way, and each rule is local, in that it inspects a bounded part of the description. A probe supplies a number, which fixes the starting configuration, and reads a number from the configuration where the run ends, or reads nothing if the run does not end. And only the line is kept. Its points group programs by the partial function from numbers to numbers that they compute, so that a program is identified with that function.

In the 1930s Church[^sn19] and Kleene[^sn19b] related λ-definability to recursiveness, and Turing introduced his machines[^sn19c] and related them to λ-definability.[^sn19d] With the standard ways of writing numbers in each, these models compute the same functions from numbers to numbers. In the modern presentation, where a run may fail to end, they put the same points on the line: the partial recursive functions, that is, the functions that some Turing machine computes, undefined where the machine does not halt. The original papers stated their results for total functions and, in Turing’s first paper, for computable sequences. That much is a theorem about the models named. The Church–Turing thesis is a further claim, that effective calculability, what a person can compute by following rules, is exactly what these models compute. In such a comparison the points group programs, and a probe runs a program from the configuration that its input fixes. A calculus adds no point if a Turing machine can carry out each of its probes: the input is written into the starting configuration by an effective rule, a fixed effective strategy chooses each next step, whether a run has ended can be decided effectively, and an ended run is read back as at most one number by an effective rule. A calculus of this kind that reaches all of these points is called universal, and every universal calculus has exactly these points on its line. This agreement is why the essay takes the usual notion as its point of reference.

**The usual notion: numbers in and out, with only the line kept.** *Turing machines, the lambda calculus, combinatory logic.* Every universal calculus whose probes a Turing machine can carry out, with a fixed effective order of steps and at most one number read back, has the same line, the partial recursive functions.

**Observation: a probe returns a cost as well as an answer.** *Complexity theory; interaction nets.* Programs with the same answers come apart. The line now depends on the calculus, though only up to a polynomial among the machine models shown to simulate one another with polynomial overhead. In well-formed interaction nets, under a well-formed table with at most one rule for each pair of agents, every run from one net to an irreducible net takes the same number of steps, so there the cost of a finished run is fixed by the net.

**Observation: a probe supplies a program, which may be called but not read.** *Computing with inputs that are themselves programs, known as computability at higher types.* Calculi that agree on numbers disagree here. There are several notions of what is computable, and no one of them has displaced the others.

**Observation: a probe may read code.** *The checkbox marked ‘Let probes read code’. Tree calculus gets the same effect by adding steps.* Sameness under probes becomes sameness after running on terms that have a value. A program can then tell different normal forms apart by inspecting their code.

**Observation: a probe may watch a run as it goes.** *Concurrent processes, in which two runs from one state need not meet again.* Two processes can show the same sequences of actions and still offer different next moves. The same steps support a range of equalities, from sameness of traces to bisimulation, one for each kind of observer.

**Observation: the answer is a proof, and there may be several.** *Dependent type theory, where a proof that two terms are equal is itself a term.* Two proofs of one equality can in turn be equal or not. The proofs have steps and fibres of their own, and the picture is repeated one level up.

**Steps: steps are allowed in both directions.** *A group given by generators and relations. Descriptions are words, and a step applies a relation.* The steps single out no normal form and no order of evaluation. In place of a value a word names a group element. For a word that names the identity in the presented group, [the fewest deletions of a relator or its inverse that take it to the empty word, where before each deletion the word may be replaced at no cost by any word equal to it in the free group, equals its area, the fewest conjugates of relators or their inverses whose product it equals in the free group](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Dehn.lean#L82 "Mechanized: Dehn.cost_eq_area"). Whether two words share a point can be undecidable.

**What is kept: the fibres are kept as well as the line.** *Associativity; the loop `ω ω`.* Loops and holes appear, as in the pentagon of bracketings. Evidence carried round a loop can come back changed: [on the bracketings, a behaviour that carries evidence backwards acts differently along the two routes](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Pentagon.lean#L99 "Mechanized: Pentagon.exists_beh_top_ne_bot").

The last line of each card reports known results, and the note gives sources. The framework adds the claim that every card is the same three ingredients and the same decision, made differently. The Mechanized section at the end lists which of these results have been mechanized, which have been argued by hand, and which links from the cards to the framework remain to be argued.

The cards on observation can also be sorted by what they give up. The probes of this essay have four properties at once. [A step in the term under test never changes a probe’s reading](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Lenses.lean#L35 "Mechanized: SKI.probes_invariant"), and [for terms with variables the same holds of each closed instance](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L127 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEqCl_of_conv"). A probe is made from the calculus’s own terms. A probe reads only what it supplied, and terms with variables of their own are compared through their closed instances. And the answer is a reading or none. In any calculus with probes as in Lean, [confluence gives the implication from sameness after running to sameness under probes](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Calculus.lean#L154 "Mechanized: Calculus.probeEq_of_conv"), compared directly, which on terms without variables is the essay’s rule. Probes made of terms compose: a context put in the hole of another is again a context, and putting a term in it is putting the term in the inner context and the result in the outer one. So [in combinatory logic, sameness under probes through closed instances, the essay’s rule, is kept by every context](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Closed.lean#L148 "Mechanized: SKI.probeEqCl_plug"), and [in any calculus with probes as in Lean, sameness under probes compared directly is kept by every context](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Calculus.lean#L188 "Mechanized: Calculus.probeEq_plug"). Reading only what was supplied is what lets probes be coarser than running. In combinatory logic they are, since [`I`, `S K K` and `S K S` agree under every probe](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Probes.lean#L349 "Mechanized: SKI.six_terms") and [lie in three fibres](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Confluence.lean#L240 "Mechanized: SKI.three_fibres"). In a confluent calculus with probes as in Lean, [a probe that may read every normal form it reaches makes sameness under probes into sameness after running on terms that have a value](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Calculus.lean#L217 "Mechanized: Calculus.codeEq_iff_conv"). With a reading or none as the answer, a probe defines a predicate, and by the first property that predicate is a type. Types in general were defined by steps alone. Two cards, the furthest from the calculus of this essay, are drawn larger here. On the card on watching a run, a trace is the sequence of actions that a run shows. Two processes are bisimilar if each can match every move of the other and remain matched afterwards. The two processes in the figure have the same traces, `a b` and `a c`. They are not bisimilar, because the first can do `a` and still offer both `b` and `c`, and the second cannot. A probe that sees only finished traces finds them equal. A probe that can stop after `a` and ask what is on offer tells them apart.

On the card on groups, take two generators, `a` and `b`, and one relation, which says that `ab` and `ba` are the same. A word is then a path on a grid, in which `a` is a move to the right and `b` a move up. A step exchanges an adjacent `ab` for `ba`, or the reverse, and it moves the path across one square. In place of a value a word has the place where its path ends, which no step changes. An observer that counts the `a`s and the `b`s reads that place. The fibre over it is the set of all paths to it.

Select a green corner, or press one of the green buttons, to exchange two letters. The corner moves across one square, which is shaded or cleared. The shaded squares lie between the two words.

The steps go both ways, so they single out no normal form, and the picture has no input, output or order of evaluation. The three ingredients are present all the same, with the observer that counts letters as the observation. Cost is present too. The number of steps a run has taken is the cost of that run, and the least cost of a run between two words is the area between their paths. Each step moves the path across one square, and a step toward the other path removes one square from between them, so no run is shorter than that area and some run has exactly that length. With other relations the same questions become hard. Whether two words name one element is called the word problem of the group, and for some groups no procedure decides it.

The last card, on keeping the fibres, changes what is kept. The picture of running can be kept at three levels. At the first, each fibre is counted as one piece, and the pieces are the points of the line. At the second, the arrowheads are forgotten and a fibre is a graph. A loop in it is a round trip from a term back to itself, such as the pentagon of bracketings read by going down one route and back up the other. Some round trips shrink to nothing. A step followed by the same step taken back cancels, and an agreed equation between two runs lets either replace the other inside a round trip. With the equation that counts the pentagon’s two routes as one, its round trip becomes one route out and the same route back, which cancels. A *hole* is a round trip that cannot be shrunk to nothing in these ways, and without that equation the pentagon is one. (A hole in this sense is not the hole of a probe’s context.) At the third, the arrowheads are kept, and a normal form is the term at which the runs of a fibre end. Cost can be added to the graph or to the picture with direction, as the length of a route.[^sn22]

[^sn22]: For length as a structure of the same kind as truth, see F. W. Lawvere, [‘Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories’](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02924844), *Rendiconti del Seminario Matematico e Fisico di Milano* 43(1), 1973, 135–166.

On a fibre without holes, keeping the graph or the direction tells a type nothing that the single point did not. A family attaches evidence to a term, a yes or a no for a predicate and a proof or a number for a richer family. On a fibre without holes, evidence carried along any two runs between the same terms arrives in the same state. A behaviour is the framework’s wider notion of type, in which each step carries evidence across in exactly one way. Over such a fibre it amounts to evidence attached to the value, and for a predicate to a yes or a no about the value. In a calculus whose rules do not overlap, such as combinatory logic, the fibre over a value is of this kind, provided that runs are counted as one by Lévy’s permutation equivalence. Two steps at separate places can be taken in either order. When a step discards an argument, a step taken inside that argument beforehand leaves nothing behind, so [the two runs of `K a (I b)` count as one](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L98 "Mechanized: SKI.kaib_one_run"). When a step copies an argument, a step taken inside it beforehand stands for the same step taken in each copy afterwards. Runs that differ in these ways count as one, and by Huet and Lévy’s normal derivation theorem any two runs from one term to its value then count as one. (The Lean development counts runs as one by a list of elementary squares, and that this list gives Lévy’s equivalence has not been mechanized.) The framework adds the step from there to the absence of holes, and it has been mechanized. Take the category whose maps are the runs of the fibre, so counted. If every term has exactly one run to one term, that term is terminal, and [inverting every run leaves a single point](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L189 "Mechanized: localization_trivial_of_terminal"): the fibre has no holes. A behaviour inverts every step, and so every run, and over such a category [the behaviours are the same as sets](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L255 "Mechanized: beh_equiv_type_of_terminal_co"), that is, as evidence about that term. Each step of a round trip, followed by the run from its end to that term, is a run to that term, and so it counts as one with the run from its start. The round trip can therefore be pulled onto that term a step at a time, and there it is empty.[^sn23] The calculus of bracketings, with its one rule `(xy)z → x(yz)`, is not of this kind. Its rule can be used at two places in `((xy)z)w` that share a bracket, neither use discards or copies the other, and [the pentagon is the hole that this overlap leaves](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Pentagon.lean#L74 "Mechanized: Pentagon.top_ne_bot_localized"), which is why it needs an equation of its own.

The equation that counts the two routes as one fills it. A coherence theorem is a proof that enough such equations have been added for the fibres to have no holes. The loop inside `K a (ω ω)` is filled already, because the K-rule discards it: as explained above, [the lap followed by the K-step counts as one run with the K-step alone](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/PermEq.lean#L97 "Mechanized: SKI.absorbed"), in either order of the lap, since [the two orders of the lap count as one](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L111 "Mechanized: SKI.omegaLoop_permEq"), so taking the K-step back cancels the lap. The loop `ω ω` has no exit to a value, and the counter on it carries information that no value holds. Every run from `ω ω` stays among terms without K, and there the count shows that [no run out of `ω ω` absorbs a lap](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/SKI/Types.lean#L118 "Mechanized: SKI.not_absorbed'"). That nothing fills the loop once every step can be taken back has been argued by hand, and only under an assumption. A round trip through the whole fibre may pass backwards through terms with K, where the count does not apply. The argument assumes that the development’s way of counting runs as one agrees with Lévy’s, so that every round trip can be rewritten as a run out followed by a run back, and that assumption is open.

Where evidence depends on the route in this way, a result belongs to the way one description was reached from another, rather than to any value. The framework names the ingredients so that notions of computation of this kind can be described in the same terms as the usual one.

## Why localization

Three older ways of giving a calculus a meaning compare terms by how they run, by the values a model gives them, or by what probes read. They differ from localization semantics in what they keep.

| Semantics | What it gives a term | What it keeps |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Its steps and runs | How the term moves |
| Denotational, with steps read as equalities in an ordered model | A value, the same for terms joined by steps | Values that depend only on the fibre |
| Observational | Its answers under every probe | Agreement under probes |
| Localization | The term, with its runs made reversible | The points of the line, and the round trips within each fibre |

Operational semantics supplies the steps, and localization semantics takes them as given. A denotational semantics gives each term a value in a model. Consider a model in which values are ordered, and two values are equal if each is below the other, such as a domain ordered by information, or a set with equality as its order. Interpret each run by an inequality from its starting value to its ending value: the empty run gives equality, and joining two runs joins their inequalities. If each step gives an inequality that holds both ways, [all the terms of a fibre, the terms joined to one another by steps taken either way, get the same value](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L272 "Mechanized: obj_eq_of_conv_of_partialOrder"). [Any two runs with the same start and finish give the same inequality](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/LocalSystems.lean#L285 "Mechanized: map_eq_of_partialOrder"), so a round trip reads the same as staying put. Such a semantics keeps at most the points of the line, one for each fibre. [The two routes round the pentagon of bracketings still differ after the steps in that example are made reversible](https://github.com/maxinelevesque/formal-computation/blob/4c583e3d6c4ed6cb3b61294f24835119c091ddd4/FormalComputation/Pentagon.lean#L74 "Mechanized: Pentagon.top_ne_bot_localized").

Observation is the third ingredient of a presentation, and it is kept apart from the steps. The probes compare terms in every context, which is the method of J. H. Morris.[^sn26] Keeping observation apart lets the probes change while the steps stay fixed, as in the section ‘When probes can read atoms’.

[^sn26]: J. H. Morris, [*Lambda-calculus models of programming languages*](https://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/64850), PhD thesis, MIT, 1968, chapter III, p. 50. Morris requires the two terms, put in any context, to have the same normal form whenever either has one. The probes here compare only the outcomes they are allowed to read.

Squier theory asks a related question about string rewriting. Its routes may use steps in either direction, and it already counts the two orders of two independent steps as one route. It asks whether finitely many further equations between routes, usable inside any surrounding word, identify every two routes with the same ends.[^sn27] Observers that see which way a step goes are left for a later essay.

[^sn27]: Squier, Otto and Kobayashi, cited above; Y. Guiraud and P. Malbos, [‘Polygraphs of finite derivation type’](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0960129516000220), *Mathematical Structures in Computer Science* 28(2), 2018, 155–201.

## Summary

Atoms are the parts from which a term is written. Probes are experiments on a term, and in this essay they read only what they supplied. Running lies between the two, and its steps join descriptions into fibres. Equality of terms can be taken as sameness of text, as membership of one fibre, or as agreement under every probe, and each of these is coarser than the one before.

A type is a predicate that no step changes. Probes give one way to define a type. In the fundamental lemma for `K`, since `K u v` steps to `u`, membership of `u` carries back to `K u v`, the term the application test asks about. Membership in a type depends only on a term’s fibre. A type does not record the length of a run, the difference between two runs, or a loop, whether or not some order of evaluation escapes it.

A presentation has three ingredients: the descriptions, the steps and the observation. The usual notion of computation is one way of choosing them, with finite and local rules that run one way, probes that deal in numbers, and only the line kept. At that setting every universal calculus, meaning one that computes all that a Turing machine can and whose probes a Turing machine can carry out, with a fixed effective order of steps and at most one number read back, has the same line, the partial recursive functions. Changing what a probe returns or may do, letting the steps run both ways, or keeping the fibres gives other notions. Keeping the fibres can distinguish runs that end at the same value, when the chosen equations between runs do not identify them.[^sn18]

[^sn18]: Three works stand close to this one. B. P. Hilken, [‘Towards a proof theory of rewriting: the simply typed 2λ-calculus’](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3975(96)80713-4), *Theoretical Computer Science* 170(1–2), 1996, 407–444, studies equations between rewrites in the simply typed 2λ-calculus. M. Fiore and P. Saville, [‘Coherence and normalisation-by-evaluation for bicategorical cartesian closed structure’](https://doi.org/10.1145/3373718.3394769), *LICS* 2020, 425–439, prove that parallel structural 2-cells in a free cartesian closed bicategory coincide (theorem 1.1). J. C. Baez and C. Williams, [‘Enriched Lawvere theories for operational semantics’](https://doi.org/10.4204/EPTCS.323.8), *EPTCS* 323, 2020, 106–135, obtains denotational semantics from operational semantics by a change of enriching category (sections 5 and 5.2).

Computed in the browser. A small reducer in the page computes, in all three calculi, the readings in the tables of probes and the point numbers beside them, and the steps between the chosen terms in the figure of running, which the figures of equalities, of leaks and of probes reuse. In tree calculus it also lays those terms out. It compiles open terms to nets, with a free wire for each variable, runs them, and reads back the term at the result port where its readback succeeds. It counts interactions, among them the counts in the table of probes, the three interactions until `a` is read in the outermost run of `K a (I b)`, and the ten interactions of every run of that net to an irreducible net, in a graph of 44 nets that it explores completely. For `ω ω` it explores five layers of nets, and it ran the net outermost first for a thousand interactions with no net repeated. These are checks of the browser’s model, and none of them is proved. The rest was made by hand: the choice of terms, their layout in combinatory logic, the grouping into fibres given to the figures of equalities and of probes, the arrows of the added rule `S K y → I` in combinatory logic, and the small panels of the pentagon, of the loops and of the fibres, in which the steps drawn were chosen by hand.

Not mechanized.

- Cited: that any two runs to a value count as one (Lévy’s theorem, in Huet and Lévy); that in combinatory logic taking the leftmost outermost redex each time reaches every value (Huet and Lévy); that the steps generate a factorization system on families (Freyd and Kelly); the results reported on the cards on cost, higher types, processes and proofs, apart from the equal length of finishing runs of nets; and the history of the Church–Turing thesis.
- Argued by hand: that the cards on proofs and on cost are instances of the framework, apart from the equal length of finishing runs of nets; that the stricter rule, under which a probe run on a term with variables of its own reads only the variables it supplied, makes a variable look like a term with no value; that on the terms of the figure of leaks, applying the probe of ‘returns its argument’ directly passes on a term exactly when it passes on all of the term’s closed instances, and likewise for ‘runs to `I`’ in tree calculus; that the loop of tree calculus has no normal form, since each of its reducts keeps a redex; in tree calculus, that the three runs of `K a (I b)` count as one and that a lap inside `K a (ω ω)` counts for nothing; that on closed terms, one of which has a value, the tree calculus’s own probes give sameness after running, and that closed instances separate `a` from `b` and keep the fibre of `a` apart from the loop; that a probe defines ‘runs to `I`’ in tree calculus, on terms without variables and, through closed instances, on the terms of the figure; that `ω ω` has no value in combinatory logic; that the loop `ω ω` stays a hole once every step can be taken back, assuming that the development’s way of counting runs as one agrees with Lévy’s; the extension of the counter to terms with K; the area count on the grid of words; that inverting the steps of the behaviour on the bracketings gives one that carries evidence forwards and changes an integer carried round the pentagon; and the two processes with the same traces that are not bisimilar.
- Open: whether the elementary squares of the development give exactly Lévy’s way of counting, on which the hole of `ω ω` in the whole fibre rests; whether the whole fibre of `ω ω` in tree calculus has a hole; whether the fibre of the net of `ω ω` has a hole, or a net in it repeats; whether a counter on nets is a behaviour on the fibre of `K a (ω ω)`; and the arguments that the cards on higher types and on concurrent processes are instances of the framework, which have not yet been made.
